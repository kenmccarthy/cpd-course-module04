Section 1 of 7

Assessment that works

~2 min read

If you teach in higher education, you've probably noticed that assessment is the part of a module students care about most. It is also the part we often design last, and the part most likely to cause stress for staff and students alike.

In the next thirty minutes, we'll look at how to design assessment that is authentic, fair and meaningful. We'll use theory where it helps, but we'll keep returning to practical decisions you can make on your own modules this semester.

By the end of this unit, you'll be able to:

  • Explain why assessment should be aligned with learning outcomes and teaching activities, and sized appropriately for the credits
  • Distinguish summative from formative assessment, and low-stakes from high-stakes assessment, and use each purposefully
  • Design a more authentic assessment task for your discipline
  • Draft a simple rubric and give feedback that students can act on
  • Make informed decisions about AI in your assessments

Pitched at NFQ Level 8. This unit is pitched at the honours bachelor degree, and we'll also look at what changes at Level 7 and Level 9. You bring something we can't supply: your own industry and professional experience. Throughout, you'll be asked to connect ideas to the workplaces your graduates enter.

Section 2 of 7

Alignment with learning outcomes

~7 min read

Start with the outcome. The most important principle in assessment design is alignment.

John Biggs called this constructive alignment (Biggs, 1996; Biggs and Tang, 2011). The idea is simple, and it has three steps.

  1. First, decide what students should be able to do by the end of the module — your learning outcome.
  2. Second, design teaching and learning activities that give students practice at that.
  3. Third, design assessment that gives students a fair chance to show that they can do it.

If those three things don't line up, students notice. They learn very quickly that what we say matters and what we assess are different things, and they study for the assessment.

Look at the verbs

As we saw in an earlier unit, learning outcomes are written with action verbs, and those verbs tell you what kind of evidence you need. Bloom's taxonomy, revised by Anderson and Krathwohl (2001), is a helpful guide. If the outcome says evaluate, a multiple-choice quiz of recall questions won't give you the evidence. If it says apply, you need a task where students apply.

Ordinary Bachelor Degree

Established knowledge in familiar contexts

Outcomes tend to focus on applying established knowledge and skills in familiar contexts.

A task might beA structured case analysis, or a guided project with clear parameters.
Honours Bachelor Degree

Autonomy, breadth and evaluation

This is our focus. The QQI award standards describe graduates who can exercise considerable autonomy, work with a breadth of knowledge, and evaluate and interpret information.

Expect tasks such asA critical analysis, a problem-based project with some ambiguity, or a professional report that needs justified recommendations.
Master's Degree

Complexity, incomplete information, originality

Here we expect graduates to deal with complexity, work with incomplete information, and contribute original thinking.

An assessment might beA research proposal, a critical review of the literature, or a consultancy-style project for a real client.

A common alignment problem. Notice that the verbs, and the amount of independence, rise through the levels. A Level 8 module is often assessed with Level 7 tasks — usually because they are quicker to mark.

How big should the assessment be?

You've now aligned your assessment with your outcomes. One more alignment question is easy to overlook: is the assessment the right size for the credits?

Every module carries ECTS credits, and credits describe the total learner effort expected. That includes lectures, labs, independent study, preparing for assessment and sitting it. Under the European Credit Transfer and Accumulation System, one credit is generally taken to represent about 20 to 25 hours of learner effort (European Commission, 2015).

If a student has about 125 hours for the whole module and attends 36 hours of class, the time left for reading, practice and assessment is fairly limited. A task that realistically needs 60 hours of work is probably too big for that module.

Three practical guidelines

  1. Count the student's hours, not yours. Estimate how long a typical student needs to complete the task, including reading, drafting and revising. Then ask whether that fits the credit value. Ask a colleague, or a recent student, to sanity-check your estimate.
  2. Keep the number of assessments proportionate. As a rule of thumb, a 5-credit module often works well with one to three assessment points. More than that tends to create over-assessment, with students bunching deadlines and staff marking more than the module can support. A 10 or 15-credit module can sustain more variety.
  3. Match size to level as well as credits. At Level 8, a smaller number of richer tasks usually works better than many short ones, because the outcomes call for analysis and evaluation. At Level 9, expect larger, more open tasks with more independence, which also means being realistic about the hours available. At Level 7, shorter, more structured tasks with clearer scaffolding are common.

A worked example

Imagine a 5-credit Level 8 module of about 125 hours. A sensible profile might be:

Weekly activity 10%
Low-stakes and formative.
Case analysis 40%
1,500 words.
Applied project or presentation 50%
Short and applied.

Compare that with five separate essays plus a final exam, which would be hard for students to complete well in the same time.

A note on word counts. Many institutions give indicative word counts or equivalencies, such as a typical number of words for an essay, or how an exam or presentation equates to written work. These vary between institutions and programmes. Check your programme document and your institution's assessment regulations, and make sure your assessment strategy is consistent with them.

Section 3 of 7

Formative, summative, low stakes and high stakes

~5 min read

People often mix up two different pairs of terms. One pair is about purpose. The other is about stakes.

The first pair is about purpose

Summative
Judges what students have achieved at the end of a period of learning. It contributes to a grade or award. This is assessment of learning.
Formative
Helps students and staff see where learning is, so that both can adjust. Black and Wiliam's (1998) review is the classic argument that formative assessment raises achievement. This is assessment for learning.
As learning
The National Forum for the Enhancement of Teaching and Learning (2017) also describes assessment as learning, where students develop their own judgement, monitoring and self-regulation.

The second pair is about stakes

Low-stakes
Carries little or no weight in the final grade.
High-stakes
Carries a lot — for example, a final exam worth 70 per cent of a module.

These two pairs are independent, which is why a matrix helps. A low-stakes quiz can be summative if it counts for a few marks. A high-stakes assessment can have formative value if students receive meaningful feedback they can apply later.

Why does this matter?

Too many high-stakes, summative-only assessments produce student anxiety and surface learning. Too few signals leave students unsure how they are doing until it is too late.

Nicol and Macfarlane-Dick (2006) argue that good formative assessment helps students become self-regulated learners. Their principles include:

  • clarifying what good performance is,
  • encouraging dialogue about learning, and
  • giving feedback that can close the gap.
Knowledge check

Which of these is the best example of formative assessment?

Practical tip: practise before the high-stakes moment. Try to make sure students get a meaningful learning opportunity before every high-stakes task. A draft, a practice run, a peer review, an in-class mini version, or an example to mark yourself all work.

Think about your industry or professional field. How do professionals get low-stakes practice and feedback before the high-stakes moment — a client presentation, a clinical placement, an audit? How could you mirror that in your module?

Section 4 of 7

Authentic assessment and adult learners

~4 min read

Authentic assessment asks students to do tasks that resemble real tasks, in realistic contexts, with realistic constraints.

Wiggins (1990) argued for assessments that are “engaging and worthy problems”. Gulikers, Bastiaens and Kirschner (2004) proposed a five-dimensional framework for authentic assessment. It looks at:

  • the task,
  • the physical context,
  • the social context,
  • the form of the assessment, and
  • the criteria.

Sambell, McDowell and Montgomery (2013) show that students respond positively when assessment feels relevant to the world beyond the classroom, which in turn tends to support deeper approaches to learning.

Why does this matter for adult learners?

Think of Knowles's work on andragogy (Knowles, 1984). Adult learners bring experience, want to know why they are learning something, and are motivated by problems they can use.

Many of our students are mature learners, part-time, upskilling or working while studying. They notice, and quickly, when an assessment has no connection to anything they will do.

Four questions to ask about any task

Examples across levels

Level 7
Produce a practical technical report from a lab task or a workplace scenario, with a clear brief.
Level 8
Prepare an evidence-informed policy brief, a project proposal with a risk analysis, or a professional portfolio with reflective commentary.
Level 9
Complete a consultancy-style project for an external partner, with a critical review of literature and presentation to stakeholders.

A word on fairness. Authentic does not mean unstructured. Make sure the task is accessible. Think about Universal Design for Learning (CAST, 2018): offer flexibility in how students demonstrate their learning where the outcome allows it. Be clear about what is being assessed and what is not. The National Forum's work on assessment emphasises inclusion and student partnership, so consider co-designing criteria with students.

Section 5 of 7

Rubrics, marking schemes and quality feedback

~5 min read

You've designed a good task. Now, how will you judge it, and how will you tell students?
Marking schemes
Typically list the points available and the expected content. They suit tasks with a defined correct answer, such as calculations or short technical answers.
Rubrics
Describe quality. They break a task into criteria and describe performance levels for each. They suit complex, open-ended tasks where judgement is needed, which is typical at Levels 8 and 9.

Reddy and Andrade (2010) review the evidence that rubrics can support learning and make judgement more transparent — provided they are used well and not just handed out.

A sample analytic rubric

Sample analytic rubric with four criteria and four performance levels
Criterion Excellent (H1) Good (H2.1) Satisfactory (H2.2/3) Not yet met
Analysis of evidence Critically evaluates multiple sources and justifies position Evaluates several sources with some critique Describes sources with limited critique Little or no analysis
Application to context Insightful, context-specific recommendations Relevant recommendations General recommendations Not related to context
Communication to audience Clear, concise, tailored to audience Clear and mostly tailored Understandable but generic Unclear
Referencing and evidence Accurate, wide range Accurate, adequate range Some errors Inadequate

Five tips for effective rubrics

  1. Use four to six criteria. More becomes unwieldy.
  2. Describe observable qualities, not vague words like “good”.
  3. Make sure the criteria link back to the learning outcomes.
  4. Share the rubric when you set the task, and talk it through.
  5. Try a short activity in which students apply the rubric to an anonymous example. This builds what Sadler (1989) called the ability to know what quality looks like.

Feedback that students can act on

Hattie and Timperley (2007) describe three questions that effective feedback answers:

1Where am I going?
2How am I going?
3Where to next?

Feedback that only justifies a grade rarely changes anything. The National Forum's work on feedback in Irish higher education stresses feedback as a dialogue and the importance of students acting on it. Carless and Boud (2018) describe this as developing students' “feedback literacy”.

Weak feedback Stronger feedback
“Needs more critical analysis. 62%.”
“Your summary of the three studies is accurate (Where am I going: critical evaluation). You describe their findings but don't yet compare their methods (How am I going). In the next assignment, try a short paragraph asking which study is most reliable and why (Where to next).”
Knowledge check · Scenario

A student receives the comment “Good work, but needs more depth.” Which revision best follows the Where to next? principle?

Practical tip: efficient feedback. Use comment banks, audio feedback, or a “two strengths, one next step” model. Ask students to respond to feedback, for example by writing a short action plan, before they see the grade.

Reflection · not scored

What do you find hardest about giving feedback? For example: time, tone, making it actionable, students not reading it — or something else.

Section 6 of 7

Assessment in the age of AI

~5 min read

Generative AI has changed what many of our traditional assessments measure. We need to respond thoughtfully, not out of panic.

A student can now produce a plausible essay, report or piece of code in minutes. Let's first look at the guidance.

What SETU's position says

  • Students' use of generative AI does not automatically constitute academic misconduct.
  • Staff are encouraged to evolve assessment design so that it emphasises authenticity, process, critical thinking and deeper learning, and it acknowledges that this may mean structural changes.
  • SETU's staff guidelines advise regularly reviewing assessment design and point to the N-TUTORR Assessment Redesign Framework as a resource.
  • Staff are also advised against relying on AI detection scores alone as evidence of misuse.

At national level, the National Academic Integrity Network (NAIN, 2023) has published guidelines for educators on generative AI, and the National Forum has published a range of resources on digital assessment and academic integrity. Check current versions, as this guidance is updated regularly.

Where to find the guidance. Links to the SETU, NAIN, QQI and National Forum material are collected under Resources and references in the final section.

Three practical responses

1. Be explicit

Tell students whether AI is permitted, restricted or prohibited in each assessment, and why, in the assessment brief. If students don't know, they will guess.

Perkins, Furze, Roe and MacVaugh (2024) propose an AI Assessment Scale with levels from “no AI” through to “AI exploration”. You can adapt something like this so your modules are consistent, and mention it in the assessment brief.

[Confirm the intermediate step names against the published AI Assessment Scale, or your institution's adaptation of it, before publishing.]

2. Redesign for process and judgement

Assessments that are harder to outsource are also usually better assessments. For example:

  • Ask for drafts, annotated process logs and reflection on decisions made.
  • Use personalised or local data, workplace case studies or a placement context. This links directly to the authentic principles in Section 4.
  • Include a short viva or oral defence at Levels 8 and 9, to confirm understanding.
  • Ask students to critique an AI-generated response, using the rubric. This builds evaluative judgement, which is a Level 8 and 9 skill.

3. Use secured assessment where it counts

For some outcomes, such as core professional competences, a supervised or live assessment is appropriate. Lodge, Howard, Bearman, Dawson and Associates (2023), writing for Australia's regulator TEQSA, argue for a mix of secured and open assessment. Not every assessment needs to be locked down, but some should be.

What not to do. Don't rely on AI detectors as proof. They are unreliable and can disadvantage some students, including those writing in a second language. If you have concerns, SETU's approach is to look at the whole picture, including how the work was produced, and to follow the formal academic integrity procedures.

Section 7 of 7

Summary and reflective activity

~2 min read

Let's pull it together.
  1. Align. Outcomes, activities and assessment need to point the same way, and the size of the task needs to fit the credits.
  2. Balance purpose and stakes. Use formative, low-stakes opportunities to prepare students for high-stakes moments.
  3. Make it authentic. Especially for adult learners, relevance drives engagement and deeper learning.
  4. Make judgement visible. Rubrics and feedback that students can act on.
  5. Respond to AI with design. Be explicit, assess process and judgement, and secure what you must.
